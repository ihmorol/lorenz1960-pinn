\begin{abstract}
We study a physics-informed neural network (PINN) approximation of a single nearly closed orbit of the Lorenz three-state system on $t\in[0,13.26446]$. We trained a single network on the full interval that produces a combined state root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 2.237. To address this long horizon problem, we divide the interval into 27 windows. Each window has a separate network and an initial state that is fixed to the predicted endpoint of the previous window. In each window, Adam reduces the residual in four stages. In each stage, we use causal weights and causal weighted loss to reduce the loss of all earlier points. Then, L-BFGS minimizes the loss using the unweighted residual mean. A numerical reference is used to monitor the training but is not used for optimization or stopping. In the seed-0 run, the windowed model gives a combined RMSE of $6.97\times10^{-5}$ on 13,265 evaluation times and a dense-grid residual mean square of $1.87\times10^{-7}$. Across five seeds, the median RMSE is $8.13\times10^{-5}$. The full-interval and windowed runs also vary in number of parameters, sampling and optimizer schedule, so we describe them as different configurations. Ablations show that separate window networks and warm start are needed. Without causal weighting the median RMSE is 2.3 times larger, but with three to five seeds this difference is not significant.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Lorenz-system model is a three-state, nonlinear ordinary differential equation (ODE) that was obtained from a reduced flow model~\cite{lorenz1960}. It has an initial-value problem which provides a simple test of the convergence of a learned continuous-time function from a specified trajectory over a long interval. A reference trajectory is provided by numerical integration. A PINN is trained with the residual of the ODE instead of the solution samples~\cite{raissi2019}. Previous works do not suggest training a long horizon problem with a traditional PINN~\cite{wang2024causal}. It couples early and late times, but the state at a later time is determined by the previous trajectory. To overcome this ordering, Wang \emph{et al.} suggested time-dependent residual weights for PINN training~\cite{wang2024causal}. An initial condition can also be met by construction using a hard trial solution~\cite{lagaris1998}.

We use both concepts. We split the time period into 27 equal windows and use a separate network for every window. The initial state of each window is fixed to the endpoint predicted by the previous window. The parameters of a trained window are copied to the next trainable window. We report one implementation and its saved result on $[0,13.26446]$, which is a nearly closed orbit for the chosen initial state. The actual training goal and window handoff are then given and compared to a saved single-network training of the same initial-value problem. The comparison is descriptive because multiple configuration options are changing simultaneously. Our contributions are: (1) a windowed PINN for one nearly closed Lorenz orbit, where 27 networks are trained in sequence with a hard endpoint handoff, warm start and staged causal weights (Section~III); (2) a seed-0 combined RMSE of $6.97\times10^{-5}$ against DOP853 and a five-seed median of $8.13\times10^{-5}$ (Section~V); (3) ablation and failure cases showing that separate window networks and warm start are needed, while the gain from causal weighting is not significant with three to five seeds (Section~V-C).



\section{Problem and Background}

\subsection{Lorenz Equations}
Our main object is a three-state nonlinear ordinary differential equation (ODE) that we need to solve. Here $x(t)$, $y(t)$ and $z(t)$ are the three states, and $\kappa$ and $\ell$ are the physical wavenumbers. We use $k$ only for the window index, which comes later. The Lorenz system general form~\cite{lorenz1960} is:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dt}
  &= \kappa\ell
     \left(\frac{1}{\kappa^2+\ell^2}
           -\frac{1}{\kappa^2}\right)yz,\\
\frac{dy}{dt}
  &= \kappa\ell
     \left(\frac{1}{\ell^2}
           -\frac{1}{\kappa^2+\ell^2}\right)xz,\\
\frac{dz}{dt}
  &= \frac{\kappa\ell}{2}
     \left(\frac{1}{\kappa^2}
           -\frac{1}{\ell^2}\right)xy.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lorenz1960}
\end{equation}

Suppose the state vector is given by $u(t)=[x(t),y(t),z(t)]^\mathsf{T}$. We put $\kappa=2$ and $\ell=1$ in~\eqref{eq:lorenz1960} and get the three coefficients.


% Physical wavenumbers: \kappa and \ell.
% Window index: k.
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_x&=2(1/5-1/4)=-1/10=-0.1,\\
a_y&=2(1-1/5)=8/5=1.6,\\
a_z&=(2/2)(1/4-1)=-3/4=-0.75.
\end{aligned}
\]

These coefficients multiply $yz$, $xz$ and $xy$, and this gives the reduced system we used in this experiment.
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}=-0.1\,yz,\qquad \dot{y}=1.6\,xz,\qquad \dot{z}=-0.75\,xy,
\label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}

If we collect the three right-hand sides, we get the vector form $du/dt=f(u)$. Here $f$ is the known ODE vector field.
\[
f(u)=[a_x yz,\,a_y xz,\,a_z xy]^\mathsf{T},\qquad f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}.
\]

The input of $f$ is a state vector with three components and the output is the rate of change of $x$, $y$ and $z$ in the same order. While training, we use this same function on the predicted state $\hat{u}_k(t)$.

For our experiment we used the coefficients $a=[a_x,a_y,a_z]^\mathsf{T}=[-0.1,1.6,-0.75]^\mathsf{T}$. We track the system starting from an initial state~\cite{matthews2024pinnde} of $u(0)=[0.5,0.75,1.0]^\mathsf{T}$ until a final time of $T=13.26446$. On this particular rounded endpoint, the reference trajectory nearly returns to its starting state. It is very similar but not exactly closed so we call this interval a nearly closed orbit and not an exact period.


\subsection{Related Work}
In this study we are making an experimental investigation of a series of well known concepts. The approach adopted is a PINN that embeds the physics residual into the training objective~\cite{raissi2019}. We use a hard trial-function design~\cite{lagaris1998} to fix our starting state exactly at the point we want. Learning long trajectories is a well-known challenge due to the ordering of the time axis so we take inspiration from the causal residual weighting technique~\cite{wang2024causal}. Lastly segmenting the timeline is based on ideas of causal sweeping and temporal decomposition~\cite{penwarden2023}. Our implementation is an investigation of the performance of the individual components in this nearly closed orbit of the Lorenz system.

Several works explain why PINN training fails. Krishnapriyan \emph{et al.} showed that PINNs can fail on simple problems and that learning the solution in short time segments helps~\cite{krishnapriyan2021}. Wang \emph{et al.} linked the failure to imbalanced gradients and to the neural tangent kernel of the loss terms~\cite{wang2021gradient,wang2022ntk}. For dynamical systems, fixed points can trap training in a wrong solution~\cite{rohrhofer2023}, and the loss landscape is ill-conditioned, so Adam followed by L-BFGS trains better than Adam alone~\cite{rathore2024}. Mattey and Ghosh also trained time segments in sequence, but with one network that is retrained on each new segment~\cite{mattey2022bcpinn}.

\section{Windowed PINN}
\subsection{Network Structure}
We used several modeling strategies to approximate the solution for Lorenz system trajectory over  $[0,T]$, where $T=13.26446$ is one nearly closed loop for the chosen initial state. We first tried one network to learn the entire trajectory at once. As it got stuck in a plateau, we tried to split the time interval in equal segments.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/plateau-results.pdf}
\caption{Single network on the full interval}
\label{fig:plateau}
\end{figure}

We chose a window width close to 0.5. In the first experiment, we used one network with 27 segmented time period to train the model sequentially. We noticed that, whenever the window is moved to the new one, the previous learning is lost or degraded. This results in a significant increase in error with RMSE of 0.350 to 0.648 over three seeds (Table~\ref{tab:ablations}). To address these issues, we introduced WPINN (Windowed PINN). We split the total time $[0,T]$ equally into 27 windows and gave each window its own network. Here $k=0,\ldots,26$ is the index of the 27 windows and $t_0,\ldots,t_{27}$ are the 28 boundary times. Each window $W_k$ has width $\Delta t=T/27$ and its own network parameters $\theta_k$.
\[
\begin{aligned}
t_k&=kT/27,\quad k=0,\ldots,27,\quad W_k=[t_k,t_{k+1}],\\
\Delta t&=T/27\approx0.4912763,\quad N_k(\,\cdot\,;\theta_k):W_k\to\mathbb{R}^{3}.
\end{aligned}
\]


Each window has its own network $N_k(t;\theta_k)$ with 1 input layer (t), 4 hidden layers of 60 neurons with tanh unit, and an output layer with 3 outputs. The output is not the state $(x,y,z)$ directly. It is a correction vector, and we get the predicted state $\hat{u}_k(t)$ from the trial solution in~\eqref{eq:trial}. With biases, the input layer has 120 parameters, the three hidden-to-hidden layers have $3\times3660$ and the output layer has 183. The total counts all 27 networks.
\[
P_{\mathrm{window}}=120+3(3660)+183=11{,}283,\; P_{\mathrm{total}}=304{,}641.
\]

For the model parameter initialization we used xavier-uniform weights and zero biases. The 27 networks do not share parameters, but neighboring windows share a boundary. The predicted endpoint of a completed window is used as the initial state of the next window. 

\subsection{Window Handoff}
As the Lorenz system is an initial value problem, we enforce the initial state of each window through a trial solution. Here $t_k$ is the starting time of window $k$, $u_{0,k}\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is its fixed starting state and $N_k(t;\theta_k)\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is the network correction.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{u}_k(t)
  &=u_{0,k}+(t-t_k)N_k(t;\theta_k),\quad t\in W_k.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:trial}
\end{equation}



At $t=t_k$, the second term is zero, so $\hat{u}_k(t_k)=u_{0,k}$ regardless of the network parameters~\cite{lagaris1998}. The first window uses the given initial state $u_{0,0}=u(0)$, and every later window uses the predicted endpoint of the last window as its initial state.
\begin{equation}
u_{0,k+1}=\hat{u}_k(t_{k+1}), \qquad k=0,\ldots,25.
\label{eq:handoff}
\end{equation}

The endpoint transfer in~\eqref{eq:handoff} and the trial solution make the predicted values continuous at the window boundaries. But the derivatives can still change suddenly at the boundaries. Separately, window $k+1$ starts from a copy of the weights of window $k$, $\theta_{k+1}\leftarrow\theta_k$ for $k=0,\ldots,25$, and then trains its own parameters. As the starting point of the later windows is predicted, it can carry error from earlier windows.

\subsection{Training Loss}
To train the network of each window, we split the time range into $n_k=1536$ uniform collocation points $t_{k,i}$, $i=1,\ldots,n_k$, including both endpoints.
\[
\begin{aligned}
t_{k,i}&=t_k+\frac{i-1}{n_k-1}(t_{k+1}-t_k),\\
n_{\mathrm{unique}}&=27(1536-1)+1=41{,}446,\quad 27\cdot1536=41{,}472.
\end{aligned}
\]


Two windows next to each other share their boundary time. So there are 41,446 unique points in total, but 41,472 points are used in training. On the global grid, each boundary point belongs to the next window, and we also add it when we train the current window. The last window has the final endpoint. While training, all the points in a single window are passed to the network at once which gives an output of shape $(1536,3)$.
Then we apply the trial solution to enforce the initial value. PyTorch automatic differentiation gives the rate of change of $x$, $y$ and $z$ with respect to time for all the collocation points. As $u_{0,k}$ is fixed while training, the product rule gives the derivative of~\eqref{eq:trial}:
\[
\frac{d\hat{u}_k}{dt}
=N_k+(t-t_k)\frac{\partial N_k}{\partial t}.
\]
% PyTorch autograd computes d\hat{u}_k/dt.
% f(\hat{u}_k) evaluates the prescribed ODE vector field.

The ODE residual function compares this derivative with $f(\hat{u}_k(t))$, which is the original ODE right-hand side at the predicted state. Both terms are vectors with three components, and their difference is the residual $r_k(t)$.
\begin{equation}
r_k(t)
=\frac{d\hat{u}_k(t)}{dt}
 -f\bigl(\hat{u}_k(t)\bigr)
\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.
\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
So the three components are $r_{k,x}=d\hat{x}_k/dt+0.1\,\hat{y}_k\hat{z}_k$, $r_{k,y}=d\hat{y}_k/dt-1.6\,\hat{x}_k\hat{z}_k$ and $r_{k,z}=d\hat{z}_k/dt+0.75\,\hat{x}_k\hat{y}_k$.

For a batch of $n_k$ times, each row is one collocation point and the three columns are $x$, $y$ and $z$. Here $\mathbf{N}_k$, $\hat{\mathbf{U}}_k$, $\dot{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}_k$, $\mathbf{F}_k$ and $\mathbf{R}_k$ mean the network outputs, trial states, time derivatives, ODE right-hand sides and residuals. Each of them is in $\mathbb{R}^{n_k\times3}$, and the time batch $\mathbf{t}_k$ is in $\mathbb{R}^{n_k\times1}$.
% Row i:
% \mathbf{N}_k: N_k(t_{k,i};\theta_k)^\mathsf{T}
% \hat{\mathbf{U}}_k: \hat{u}_k(t_{k,i})^\mathsf{T}
% \dot{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}_k: (d\hat{u}_k/dt)(t_{k,i})^\mathsf{T}
% \mathbf{F}_k: f(\hat{u}_k(t_{k,i}))^\mathsf{T}
% \mathbf{R}_k: r_k(t_{k,i})^\mathsf{T}

For each ordered collocation time $t_{k,i}$, we take the mean of the three squared residual components. This gives one non-negative loss value $L_{k,i}$ for each point instead of three components:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L_{k,i}
  &=\tfrac{1}{3}\left\|r_k(t_{k,i})\right\|_2^2=\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(r_{k,x}^2+r_{k,y}^2+r_{k,z}^2\bigr)(t_{k,i}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pointloss}
\end{equation}

For a long horizon time dependent ODE system, residual loss alone may not be sufficient. That is why we used causal weights and causal weighted loss. The causal weight at a time depends on the accumulated residual loss at earlier times~\cite{wang2024causal}. Here $S_{k,i}$ is the sum of the point losses at all earlier times of the same window. The first point has no earlier point, so $S_{k,1}=0$ and its causal weight is one.
\begin{equation}
w_{k,i}
=\exp\left(-\epsilon S_{k,i}\right)
=\exp\left(-\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}L_{k,j}\right).
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
So $0<w_{k,i}\leq1$. Then we multiply each point loss with its causal weight and take the mean over all $n_k$ points. This gives one loss value for the window in the current $\epsilon$ stage:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{k,\epsilon}
=\frac{1}{n_k}
 \sum_{i=1}^{n_k}w_{k,i}L_{k,i}
\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}.
\label{eq:causalloss}
\end{equation}
% k: window index; i: current point; j: earlier point.
% \epsilon: scalar causal-weighting parameter.
% \mathcal{L}_{k,\epsilon}: one scalar objective at current parameters.
% Weights are recomputed, then detached for gradient calculation.

Here $k$ is the window, $i$ is the current point and $j<i$ means the earlier points of $i$. $\mathbf{L}_k,\mathbf{w}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{n_k}$ have one loss and one weight for each point. $\epsilon$ in the equation means how strongly the earlier losses reduce the later weights. We used four $\epsilon$ stages: $0.01$, $0.1$, $1$, and $10$. When the earlier losses go down, the later weights go up towards one. The weights are recomputed during training, and they are fixed (detached) while the Adam optimizer calculates the gradient. The causal weights also depend on the number and spacing of the collocation points~\cite{wang2024causal}.

Only using the ODE residual is not enough for a long horizon system like the Lorenz system. Especially for the windowed algorithm, early times should be fitted before late times, as the errors accumulate and backtracking is not possible. Therefore, later states depend on the earlier states, if earlier error is reduced, possibly the later error can be reduced as well. Equations~\eqref{eq:pointloss}--\eqref{eq:causalloss} do this with the point losses and the causal weights. The next subsection explains the stopping rules of the stages and the unweighted L-BFGS refinement steps.

\subsection{Training Procedure}

The causal loss defined above is minimized separately in each window. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the sequence across the windows. Algorithm~\ref{alg:training} gives the training steps. In each window an Adam optimizer and a StepLR scheduler are created. The learning rate starts at $10^{-3}$ and it is multiplied by 0.9 for every 1,000 updates across all four $\epsilon$ stages of the window. There is a 4,000 update limit for each stage. After every Adam update the code recalculates the causal weights at all collocation points of the window. If the minimum causal weight of all 1536 points of the window is above 0.99 after an update, then it moves to the next $\epsilon$ stage. If it is below 0.99 and it reaches the update limit then it moves to the next stage and records failure.

When all four stages are completed, L-BFGS tries to minimize the loss using unweighted residual mean up to 1,000 iterations. For L-BFGS, we remove the causal weights and use the plain mean of the point losses.
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{raw}}_k
=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_i L_{k,i}
=\frac{1}{3n_k}\sum_i\|r_k(t_{k,i})\|_2^2.
\]

To keep track of training, we save a snapshot every 50 updates.

The code saves the parameters of each window, transfers the endpoint state, and copies the parameters to the next trainable window. The config file stores the configuration, initial states, training history, next window and the PyTorch and NumPy states. A run with a different configuration is rejected. DOP853 values are captured at fixed evaluation points. They do not enter the loss, gradient, or stopping test. After training, a dedicated custom function collects all the artifacts and evaluates the final piecewise model and its residual on the collocation and evaluation grids. Finally, a summary function computes error and invariant measures from these arrays. The reported dense residual is one of these measures.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Sequential window training}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Ordered windows $W_0,\ldots,W_{26}$ and their uniform time points
\State Set $u_{0,0}\gets[0.5,0.75,1.0]^\mathsf{T}$
\For{$k=0,\ldots,26$}
  \If{$k>0$}
    \State Set $u_{0,k}\gets\hat{u}_{k-1}(t_k)$
    \State Initialize $\theta_k\gets\theta_{k-1}$
  \EndIf
  \State Add $t_{k+1}$ to the residual points if $k<26$
  \For{$\epsilon\in(0.01,0.1,1,10)$}
    \For{at most 4,000 Adam steps}
      \State Use ordered times $t_{k,i}$, $i=1,\ldots,n_k$
    \State Form $\hat{u}_k$ by~\eqref{eq:trial}; get $d\hat{u}_k/dt$ by autograd
    \State Form $r_k$ by~\eqref{eq:residual} and $L_{k,i}$ by~\eqref{eq:pointloss}
    \State Compute $w_{k,i}$ by~\eqref{eq:weights}; detach weights
      \State Backpropagate~\eqref{eq:causalloss}; Adam then StepLR
      \If{verified post-update $\min_i w_{k,i}>0.99$}
        \State \textbf{break}
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State Record whether the threshold was met or capped
  \EndFor
  \State Run L-BFGS on the unweighted residual loss
  \State Save window model, endpoint state, and config record
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation}
At each evaluation time $t_j$, $j=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eval}}$, we subtract the reference state from the predicted state and get the error vector $e(t_j)\in\mathbb{R}^3$. This error is not the same as the ODE residual.
\[
\begin{aligned}
e(t_j)&=\hat{u}(t_j)-u_{\mathrm{ref}}(t_j)=[e_x,e_y,e_z]^\mathsf{T}(t_j).
\end{aligned}
\]
For the combined RMSE, we take the mean of the squared error norms over all evaluation times and then the square root. It is also equal to the square root of the sum of the three squared component RMSEs.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{RMSE}_2
  &=\left(
      \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}
      \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}
      \|e(t_j)\|_2^2
     \right)^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
For each state $c\in\{x,y,z\}$ we also report MAE, RMSE and the maximum absolute error. $E_{\mathrm{mean}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{max}}$ are the mean and the maximum of the error norm.
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{MAE}_c&=\tfrac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}\textstyle\sum_j|e_c(t_j)|,\quad \operatorname{Max}_c=\max_j|e_c(t_j)|,\\
\operatorname{RMSE}_c&=\bigl(\tfrac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}\textstyle\sum_j e_c(t_j)^2\bigr)^{1/2},\quad c\in\{x,y,z\}.
\end{aligned}
\]



The dense-grid residual mean square is the average of the three residual components squared, taken over the times of the evaluation. So it has a factor of $1/(3n_{\mathrm{eval}})$, but the combined state RMSE is not divided by three.
\[
\operatorname{MSE}_{r,\mathrm{eval}}
=\frac{1}{3n_{\mathrm{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{eval}}}\|r(t_j)\|_2^2.
\]
 The comparison run uses the same network depth and width on the entire interval $[0,T]$, and we compare their final outputs.

\subsection{Ablation Protocol}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations} shows the ablation and failure-case runs. All runs use the same initial state, coefficients, float64 precision, interval and DOP853 reference grid. The candidate is our windowed PINN from Section~III, and we ran it with seeds 0--4. Seed 0 is the run we reported in Tables~\ref{tab:errors} and~\ref{tab:comparison}. For the ablations we used seeds 1--3. In the no-causal case we removed the causal weights and trained each window for 1,300 Adam steps (35,100 in total). This is close to the candidate, which used 34,768 steps on average. In the no-warm-start case we kept the candidate schedule but did not copy the parameters of the previous window. The shared-network case uses the no-causal schedule, but one network is trained for all 27 windows one after another. In the causal single-network case we used causal weights on one network for the full interval, with the same grid and budget as the full-interval run: 39,793 LHS points, 40,000 Adam steps and at most 5,000 L-BFGS iterations. Some runs were on CPU and some on GPU, so we compare Adam steps and not wall time.

\subsection{Failure Cases}
We used two failure cases. The shared-network case checks if one network can keep what it learned in the earlier windows. The causal single-network case checks if causal weighting alone can fix the error of the full-interval network. We judge a run only by its final error against DOP853. All the runs completed training, so a large error here means a poor prediction and not a stopped job. When a stage reaches the 4,000-step cap we record it, but this alone does not mean the trajectory failed. We did not use any success cutoff to pick runs, and Table~\ref{tab:ablations} shows every completed seed.

\subsection{Ablation and Failure Results}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations} shows the ablation results. The median RMSE of the candidate is $8.13\times10^{-5}$, and three of the five seeds are below $10^{-4}$. Without causal weights the median is about 2.3 times larger, but the difference is not significant (Mann--Whitney $p=0.196$), and for seed 2 the no-causal run has lower RMSE than the candidate. Without warm start, all three seeds have larger RMSE than the candidate with the same seed. The mean Adam steps also rise to 102,989, and 17--19 of the 108 stages reach the cap, while the candidate has only 1--2. When one network is reused for all windows, the RMSE is between 0.350 and 0.648. With separate networks and the same no-causal schedule it is between $1.75\times10^{-4}$ and $5.16\times10^{-4}$. This matches what we noticed in Section~III, that the previous learning is lost or degraded when one network moves to a new window. The causal single network gives 0.275 in its one run. This is lower than the full-interval run but still far from the windowed runs. These runs support the combination of windows, warm start and causal weights that we tested for this orbit. But with three to five seeds, they do not show that causal weighting always improves the accuracy.

\section{Conclusion}

Our 27-window PINN passes every check on the nearly closed Lorenz-system interval $[0,13.26446]$. The seed-0 run had a combined state RMSE of $6.97\times10^{-5}$ in comparison to DOP853, and the median over five seeds is $8.13\times10^{-5}$. A separate network trained on the full interval recorded an RMSE of 2.237. This research applies to only one initial state and one coefficient set. Other initial states and other coefficient sets may result in different behavior. Future research will focus on matched configurations and initial states and generalize to problems with specified boundary conditions at each end.
