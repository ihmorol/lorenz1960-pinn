# PINNs for long-horizon ODE systems: the failures are diagnosed, the fixes are partial, and precision is the under-reported lever

*Deep-research survey, 21 September 2026. Written for the Lorenz-1960 FYDP; the "this project" evidence cited below refers to the runs under `runs/` on branch `feat/long-horizon-runs`.*

## Abstract

A 4x60 tanh physics-informed neural network (PINN) that reproduces the Lorenz-1960 trajectory on t in [0, 1] to 1.7e-5 RMSE degrades to 1.6e-3 on [0, 10], spends 8000 of 20 000 Adam epochs in a flat basin whose curve is the system's fixed point, and cannot be improved by L-BFGS in float32. This survey asks what the literature says about each of those observations. Four search perspectives (failure mechanisms and fixes; architecture and scaling; optimisers and precision; critics and alternatives) produced 63 verified works, 2018 to 2026. Three findings matter. First, the plateau is a documented phenomenon with a name: fixed points of the ODE create local optima of the residual loss (Rohrhofer et al. 2023), and the mean-over-time loss cannot distinguish that optimum from the true solution because it violates causality (Wang et al. 2024); causal weighting, time-marching and fixed-point regularisation are the three literature responses, none of which is uniformly dominant. Second, the accuracy floor of about 1e-3 on the long window is consistent with what the loss-landscape and precision literature predicts for Adam-only float32 training; the ill-conditioning explanation (Rathore et al. 2024) and the precision-stall explanation (Xu et al. 2025) both predict that L-BFGS stops early in float32, and both point to float64 plus a second-order finisher, or multi-stage networks, as the route below 1e-4. Third, the two most-cited architecture recipes (Fourier features with normalised inputs; modified MLP / PirateNets) are validated on PDEs, not on three-state ODEs, and this project's ablation shows normalisation alone hurts on [0, 10], so the recipe transfers only as a package. The survey closes with a ranked, evidence-graded recipe and four gaps the retrieval found empty, including the absence of any published PINN study of Lorenz-1960.

## 1. Introduction

The observations that motivate this survey are concrete. On t in [0, 10] the project's baseline PINN reached a loss of 3.7e-4 within 500 epochs and stayed there until epoch 8000 while its predicted curve sat at (x, y, z) approximately (0.03, 1.68, 0.02), a point where the Lorenz-1960 right-hand side vanishes; it then escaped and converged to a residual RMS of 4.6e-4 and a trajectory RMSE of 1.6e-3, one hundred times worse than the same network on [0, 1]. Appending L-BFGS changed the loss from 7.013e-8 to 7.012e-8 in 13 function evaluations before stopping. Normalising the input to [-1, 1] made the result three times worse. A sequential "trapezoid walk" implemented in the repository failed completely (RMSE 2.06). Existing surveys of PINNs [2][76][50] catalogue methods but do not tell a practitioner which of these symptoms has which cause, or which remedies have evidence on low-dimensional ODE systems rather than on the PDE benchmarks where most PINN research is done. That is what this report adds.

The research questions are:

- **RQ1.** What causes the long-horizon failure observed here (the fixed-point plateau, and error growing with t), and which training strategies fix it with the strongest evidence?
- **RQ2.** Which architecture and input/output scaling choices give the best accuracy for low-dimensional dynamical systems, and what accuracy floor do optimiser and precision choices impose?
- **RQ3.** What is the defensible 2024-2026 recipe for a small ODE PINN, and where does the evidence run out?

Section 2 gives the method and corpus. Section 3 lays out the taxonomy. Sections 4 and 5 answer RQ1 (mechanisms, then remedies); Sections 6 and 7 answer RQ2 (architecture, then optimisation and precision); Section 8 covers critics and alternatives; Section 9 synthesises across branches and maps each of this project's observations onto the literature; Section 10 lists open problems; Section 11 answers the RQs.

## 2. Methodology

Four search perspectives were run in parallel by independent scouts, each seeded with the sub-topics of the brief and instructed to verify every candidate by retrieving its title and authors before returning it: (i) failure modes and long-horizon fixes; (ii) architecture, feature embeddings, loss weighting, recipes and benchmarks; (iii) optimisers, precision floors, error bounds; (iv) critical evaluations, surveys, Lorenz-type applications, adjacent alternatives, and seed-variance reporting. Keyword patterns followed method x domain ("causal training" + "PINN"; "time marching" + "PINN"), mechanism x task ("fixed point" + "physics loss"), and field + survey/benchmark ("PINN benchmark 2024", "PINN review 2025"); a second round narrowed on terms returned by the first (R3 sampling, PirateNets, NNCG, energy natural gradient, bc-PINN, HCS-PINN). Sources were arXiv, journal pages, PMLR, OpenReview, ADS and OSTI mirrors when publisher pages refused access. Time range: foundational works from 1998 and 2018-2019, with emphasis on 2023-2026.

Inclusion: the work must address training or accuracy of PINNs (or a close neural ODE-solver relative) on time-dependent problems, and must be retrievable with matching title and authors. Exclusion: works whose authors could not be confirmed (four arXiv entries and one MDPI article were dropped on this rule), and neural-ODE / data-driven forecasting works that do not solve an initial-value problem from the equation alone (one Lorenz-63 SciML paper was kept only as an adjacent comparison). Nine of the load-bearing references were re-verified by the author of this report against the arXiv abstract or full text, independently of the scouts. Final corpus: 63 works.

Evidence standards follow the CS/AI convention: benchmark relative L2 errors, ablations, seed protocols, and reproducibility notes are reported where the abstract or page states them; numbers not visible on a retrieved page are not quoted. Where this project's own runs bear on a claim they are cited as "this project" with the run name, and treated as a single-seed, single-system data point.

The taxonomy is organised by *where the remedy acts* (loss, time domain, architecture and inputs, optimiser and precision, or replacing the PINN), crossed with *which failure mechanism it targets*. That choice follows from RQ1 and RQ2 being about mechanism and remedy respectively, and it makes the empty cells visible.

## 3. Taxonomy

Four failure mechanisms recur across the corpus:

- **M1, fixed-point optima.** The residual loss has local minima at equilibria of the ODE; a flat curve at a fixed point has zero residual everywhere except where the initial condition forces a bend.
- **M2, causality violation.** A loss averaged over time lets later collocation points be "satisfied" by a wrong solution before earlier points are, so error propagates forward and the optimiser finds a trivial or shifted solution.
- **M3, ill-conditioning and precision.** Differential operators make the loss Hessian ill-conditioned; first-order optimisers crawl, quasi-Newton line searches fail, and in float32 the residual saturates at the round-off floor before the solution is accurate.
- **M4, spectral bias and capacity.** The network learns low frequencies first and may lack the effective bandwidth to represent an oscillation over a long window; input scaling changes this in either direction.

Remedies act at five levels. The table marks which mechanism each remedy is argued to address, with the works assigned to that cell; a dash marks a cell with no retrieved work.

Two cells deserve comment before the branches. No architecture-level remedy for fixed-point optima was retrieved: every M1 fix acts on the loss, the window or the sampling. And no work applies second-order or float64 optimisation *specifically* to the fixed-point escape; the precision literature ([5], [35]) is about the accuracy floor after escape, not about the escape itself. Both are gaps taken up in Section 10.

[5], which reinterprets what the fixed-point literature calls a local minimum as a float32 stall. Section 9 confronts those two readings with this project's data.

## 4. Why long-horizon ODE PINNs fail: fixed points, causality, precision

This branch collects the diagnostic literature for RQ1 and tests each mechanism against the project's observations.

**The fixed-point mechanism is the one that matches the plateau.** Rohrhofer et al. [6] show, on dynamical-system benchmarks, that "the loss landscape exhibits local optima that are shaped by the presence of fixed points", and that under "initial conditions close to fixed points or long simulation times" those optima can outperform the desired solution in loss. That is exactly the epoch-3000 state of this project's baseline: the network's curve bends from u0 to (0.03, 1.68, 0.02), where the Lorenz-1960 right-hand side (-0.10yz, 1.60xz, -0.75xy) vanishes, residual 6.5e-2 on t < 2 falling to 3e-3 on t > 6, and a loss of 3.7e-4 that reads as converged. Steger et al. [37] document the same behaviour on a chaotic double pendulum, where the PINN "cheats" by shifting to a lower-frequency solution that marginally violates the initial condition or the physics locally; Krishnapriyan et al. [1] place the origin in the loss landscape rather than in network expressivity and report vanilla-PINN relative errors of 75% at convection coefficient 20, 96% at 40, and 98% for reaction coefficient 5, averaged over at least ten seeds. The follow-up by Babic et al. [7] turns the diagnosis into a remedy by penalising solutions at *unstable* fixed points, tested on Lotka-Volterra and Van der Pol; whether Lorenz-1960's fixed points on the x = z = 0 axis are unstable in the sense their stability-theory penalty needs is not established by the retrieved abstract, so the transfer to this project is plausible rather than demonstrated.

**The causality mechanism explains why the mean loss cannot see the fixed-point optimum.** Wang, Sankaran and Perdikaris [8] argue that standard PINN losses cannot "respect the spatio-temporal causal structure that is inherent to the evolution of physical systems", and identify this as "a fundamental limitation and a key source of error"; their remedy, weighting each time block by the exponentiated cumulative loss of earlier blocks, is reported to be the first PINN success on the chaotic Lorenz-63 system, the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation in its chaotic regime and turbulent Navier-Stokes. Penwarden et al. [9] generalise the idea into a unified framework of causal sweeping that includes time-marching PINNs and XPINNs, and note that poor local minima give "an inferior, and sometimes trivial, solution when solving forward time-dependent PDEs with no data"; Daw et al. [16] name the same outcome a "propagation failure", a trivial solution with an imbalanced residual field, and Wu et al. [38] prove that such failures arise from low gradient correlation between nearby collocation points. These four are independent formulations of one observation: M1 and M2 are the same failure seen from the loss-landscape side and from the training-dynamics side, and this project's residual profile (large near t = 0, near zero later) is the signature all four describe.

**The precision mechanism is the dissenting reading.** Xu et al. [5] argue that in float32 "the LBFGS optimizer prematurely satisfies its convergence test, freezing the network in a spurious failure phase", and that upgrading to float64 lets "vanilla PINNs solve PDEs without any failure modes"; they reframe failures as "precision induced stalls rather than inescapable local minima". Rathore et al. [4] give the mechanism at the optimiser level: the differential operators in the residual make the loss ill-conditioned, Adam crawls in narrow valleys, and L-BFGS's strong-Wolfe line search can return a zero step with the gradient norm still around 1e-2 to 1e-3. This project's L-BFGS run reproduces the second observation exactly (13 evaluations, no change), which supports [4] and [5] on the *accuracy floor*. It does not support the stronger claim in [5] that the plateau itself is a precision artefact: the plateau occurred under Adam alone, at a loss of 3.7e-4, five orders of magnitude above float32 round-off, and it ended by escape rather than by stalling. Under the conditions of this project, the fixed-point and causality readings explain the plateau and the precision reading explains the floor; the two are complementary, not competing.

**Spectral bias is the mechanism the project's own ablation touched.** Wang, Yu and Perdikaris [3] show with the neural tangent kernel that PINNs learn along dominant eigen-directions and suffer a convergence-rate disparity between loss terms; Wang, Wang and Perdikaris [18] and Tancik et al. [19] show that Fourier features widen the learnable bandwidth in low-dimensional inputs. This project's normalise-input ablation is a negative result in that framework: mapping t to [-1, 1] put 97% of first-layer pre-activations inside the linear region of tanh, halving the effective bandwidth, and RMSE rose from 1.6e-3 to 5.3e-3. The literature predicts this: [18] and [22] pair normalisation with Fourier features precisely because a normalised input into a Xavier-initialised tanh layer is a low-pass network.

*Takeaway:* the plateau is M1/M2 with strong, independently converging evidence; the floor is M3 with two independent explanations that agree on the fix; M4 is real but only bites when input scaling is changed without a compensating feature map.

## 5. Remedies that act on the loss or the time domain

This branch weighs the RQ1 remedies. Three families have head-to-head evidence on time-dependent problems; none is uniformly best, and the conditions that decide between them are the window length and whether error accumulation or optimisation failure is the binding constraint.

**Causal weighting is the lightest change and the best-validated on chaotic ODEs.** Wang et al. [8] apply it to Lorenz-63 with a single network and report success where prior PINNs "have not been successful"; Es'kin et al. [23] use a delta-function variant with one- or two-hidden-layer networks and deterministic initialisation, reaching what they call competitive or state-of-the-art accuracy on ODE examples that include the Lorenz system; Li et al. [40] combine causal ordering with implicit time-differencing and frame-to-frame transfer, reporting 4-40x efficiency over existing PINN formulations; Kapoor et al. [39] find that the causal loss works on large space-time domains but generalises poorly to new parameters without transfer learning. This project's causal run (eps annealed from 1e-2 to 100 over 32 chunks) escaped the fixed-point basin only 100 epochs earlier than the baseline and improved RMSE by 3%, which is far below what [8] reports on Lorenz-63. The condition difference is visible in the weights: with block losses of order 4e-4 the causal weights stayed above 0.37 even at the eps ceiling, so the weighting never became a hard ordering. Wang et al.'s reported successes use their full recipe (Fourier features, modified MLP, learning-rate schedule, eps schedule) [8][25]; the evidence therefore supports causal weighting as part of a package, and does not support it as a drop-in fix on a vanilla network.

**Time-domain decomposition is the most robust and the most error-accumulating.** Krishnapriyan et al. [1] introduce sequence-to-sequence training over time windows as one of two remedies (with curriculum) and show it removes their failure cases; Mattey and Ghosh [10] retrain one network over successive segments while constraining it to previously learned segments (bc-PINN), reporting it "significantly more accurate and efficient than PINN" on Allen-Cahn and Cahn-Hilliard; Roy and Castonguay [13] enforce continuity between segments exactly in the ansatz (HCS-PINN) and report gains over soft-constrained sequential PINNs on advection, Allen-Cahn, KdV, wave and chaotic jerky dynamics; Guo et al. [12] pre-train on small time sub-domains and use the result as initialisation and extra supervision for larger ones; Meng et al. [11] parallelise short windows under a coarse solver; Wight and Zhao [15] grow the window adaptively while resampling 200 points per iteration on top of 2000; Haitsiukevich and Ilin [14] grow the window only where an ensemble agrees; Baty [46] applies a growing window with a moving collocation grid to stiff ODEs; Han and Temuer [47] and Hossain et al. [48] use piecewise sub-interval networks with parameter transfer specifically for long-interval ODE initial-value problems, the latter on Lorenz. This project's marching run (ten unit windows, fresh network each, previous end state as initial condition) never plateaued, with every window converging within its 5000 epochs, but finished at RMSE 3.1e-3, twice the baseline, because each window inherits the previous end-state error. That is the trade the decomposition literature describes and [13] targets: marching removes the optimisation failure and replaces it with accumulation, which hard continuity enforcement and parameter transfer reduce but do not remove.

**Adaptive sampling attacks the same failure from the collocation side.** Daw et al. [16] retain, resample and release collocation points so that they accumulate where the residual is high, framed explicitly as a fix for propagation failure; Wu et al. [17] compare six uniform samplers against residual-based adaptive refinement and distribution (RAR, RAD) across PDEs and find RAD consistently best; Andersen and Matsubara [36] argue from residual visualisation that failure modes are overfitting to the collocation set, "the loss is minimized on the collocation points, but not elsewhere", and remove them with a double-backpropagation regulariser using substantially fewer points. This project measured a residual generalisation gap of 1.02 between its 3000 Latin-hypercube points and 1001 unseen points, so [36]'s overfitting mechanism is not active here at the final state; the sampling remedies would act during the plateau, where this project has no measurement. Their evidence is on PDEs; no retrieved sampling paper evaluates on a three-state ODE.

| Remedy | Representative works | Mechanism targeted | Evidence base | Result on this project, [0, 10] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Causal weighting | [8][9][23][39][40] | M2 (and M1 via ordering) | Lorenz-63, KS, NS; ODE suites in [23] | RMSE 1.5e-3 vs 1.6e-3; plateau unchanged |
| Time marching / sequential | [1][10][11][12][13][14][15][46][47][48] | M1, M2 | Allen-Cahn, Cahn-Hilliard, KdV, stiff ODEs, Lorenz [48] | RMSE 3.1e-3; no plateau; error accumulates |
| Fixed-point regulariser | [7] | M1 | Lotka-Volterra, Van der Pol (+2) | not run |
| Adaptive sampling | [15][16][17][36] | M2, overfitting | PDE benchmarks | not run |

*Caption: on this project's single system and seed, marching is the only remedy that removed the plateau, causal weighting the only one that improved accuracy, and neither did both; the literature reports larger gains for both, always inside a fuller recipe.*

## 6. Architecture and input scaling for low-dimensional dynamics

This branch covers the RQ2 architecture evidence. The dominant recipes come from PDE work by one group and are validated as packages; the ODE-specific evidence points the other way, toward smaller networks.

**Feature embeddings and input scaling are inseparable.** Tancik et al. [19] show that a random Fourier mapping of a low-dimensional input turns the network's kernel into a stationary one with tunable bandwidth; Wang, Wang and Perdikaris [18] transfer this to PINNs with spatio-temporal Fourier embeddings and explain the effect through NTK eigenvector bias; Wang et al. [20] add random weight factorisation as a reparameterisation that gives each neuron its own effective learning rate and mitigates spectral bias; Fazliani et al. [22] show that a single hidden layer with Fourier features (SAFE-NET) matches larger architectures on PDE benchmarks with 53% fewer parameters than competing feature-engineering methods and epochs 95% faster, reporting relative L2 errors such as 1.21e-3 on the wave equation and 1.21e-4 on diffusion. None of these evaluates a three-state ODE, but the mechanism is dimension-independent and this project's ablation supplies the missing half of the argument: normalising the input without an embedding lowered accuracy three-fold, which is what a stationary low-bandwidth kernel predicts. The evidence therefore supports "normalise and embed" and contradicts "normalise alone".

**Deep architectures earn their depth only with residual gating; shallow ones may suffice for ODEs.** Wang, Teng and Perdikaris [24] introduce the modified MLP with multiplicative gating alongside learning-rate annealing; Wang et al. [21] show with PirateNets that plain deep PINNs degrade with depth and that adaptive residual gates initialised to make the network shallow restore the benefit of depth; the Expert's Guide [25] consolidates these into a default of modified MLP, Fourier features, RWF, grad-norm weighting and causal weighting, with Adam at 1e-3 and exponential decay, and releases the JAX-PI code. Against this, Es'kin et al. [23] argue that for ODEs one or two hidden layers with deterministic initialisation reach competitive or state-of-the-art accuracy, and Fazliani et al. [22] reach PDE-benchmark accuracy with one hidden layer. The two positions are not in conflict once the target is fixed: the deep recipes are validated on multi-scale PDEs, the shallow ones on ODEs and smooth PDEs. For a three-state periodic ODE the retrieved evidence favours the shallow-with-features direction, and this project's 4x60 baseline sits between the two camps without belonging to either.

**KANs are not yet a safe substitute.** Shukla et al. [26] compare MLP and KAN representations under a FAIR protocol and find B-spline KANs less accurate and less efficient, with low-order Chebyshev KANs matching PINNs but liable to diverge across seeds and at higher polynomial order; Rigas et al. [49] fix the depth instability of Chebyshev PIKANs with a basis-agnostic initialisation and residual gating; Toscano et al. [50] review the PIKAN line as one of the open directions rather than a settled default. For this project the KAN evidence is a reason not to switch: the seed-divergence finding in [26] is the failure this project cannot afford to add.

**Loss weighting between terms is largely moot under a hard initial condition.** NTK-based weights [3], learning-rate annealing [24], self-adaptive per-point weights [51], residual-based attention [27] (reported to converge about one order of magnitude faster than vanilla PINNs), balanced residual decay rates [28] and dual-cone gradient descent [29] all balance the residual term against boundary or initial terms. This project imposes the initial condition exactly through u = u0 + g(t) N(t), so there is one loss term and the inter-term balancing has nothing to act on; the per-point variants ([27], [28], [51]) remain applicable and overlap in intent with causal and R3 weighting.

**The hard-constraint form itself is under-studied for long windows.** Lagaris et al. [52] introduced the trial solution as an initial-condition-satisfying part plus a parameter-free factor times the network; Lu et al. [53] and Sukumar and Srivastava [54] develop exact imposition for boundary conditions with distance functions; Roy and Castonguay [13] do the same for temporal continuity between windows. No retrieved work studies how the factor g(t) should scale with the window length. This project's audit found the consequence: with g = (t - t0)/(tf - t0) the network must output N(0) = 10 f(u0) = (-0.75, 8.0, -2.8) on [0, 10], which the final snapshot confirms to two decimals, so the required output range grows linearly with the window. Lagaris's original form uses g = t - t0 without normalisation, which keeps N(0) = f(u0). The evidence for changing g is mechanistic and single-project; it is the gap most directly actionable here.

| Choice | Works | Validated on | Direction of evidence for a 3-state ODE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fourier features + normalised input | [18][19][22][25] | PDEs | supported as a pair; normalisation alone contradicted (this project) |
| Modified MLP / PirateNets | [21][24][25] | multi-scale PDEs | not tested on ODEs; depth not shown necessary |
| One or two hidden layers, deterministic init | [22][23] | ODE suites, smooth PDEs | supported |
| KAN / cKAN | [26][49][50] | PDEs, operator learning | seed divergence reported; not recommended |
| Hard IC with g = (t - t0)/T | [52][53][54] | (form only) | output range grows with T (this project); unstudied |

*Caption: the architecture evidence for low-dimensional ODEs favours shallow networks with feature embeddings over the deep PDE recipes, and leaves the scaling of the hard-constraint factor unexamined.*

## 7. Optimisers and the precision floor

This branch answers the accuracy-floor half of RQ2. The evidence converges on three statements: Adam alone floors near 1e-3 to 1e-4 relative error; L-BFGS helps only if it can take a step; below 1e-5 the tools are second-order methods, float64, or multi-stage networks.

**Adam plus L-BFGS is the baseline, and its failure is understood.** Rathore et al. [4] report tuned relative L2 errors on a convection problem of 5.96e-2 for Adam, 8.26e-3 for L-BFGS, 4.19e-3 for Adam then L-BFGS and 1.94e-3 with their Nyström-preconditioned Newton-CG, a 14x improvement over Adam, and explain the L-BFGS stall as the Wolfe line search failing to find a positive step in an ill-conditioned valley; Xu et al. [5] attribute the same stall to float32 satisfying the convergence test prematurely and show it disappears in float64; Shao et al. [55] switch precision from float32 to float64 when L-BFGS curvature demands it and match or slightly exceed full-float64 accuracy at lower cost on four failure-mode benchmarks and one ODE; Hayford et al. [56] show pure float16 diverges while float16/32 mixed precision retains accuracy, which bounds the precision argument from below. This project's L-BFGS behaviour (stop after 13 evaluations at a loss of 7e-8 in float32) is the phenomenon [4] and [5] describe, and the two explanations are not exclusive: [4] says the step cannot be found, [5] says the change cannot be measured, and at 7e-8 in float32 both are true.

**Second-order and natural-gradient methods move the floor by orders of magnitude, at cost.** Müller and Zeinhofer [31] show energy natural gradient descent reaches errors "several orders of magnitude smaller" than gradient descent or Adam; Jnini et al. [32] reach close to single-precision accuracy on Navier-Stokes with a matrix-free Gauss-Newton natural gradient; Jnini and Vella [33] scale a dual natural-gradient method to 12.8 million parameters with one to three orders lower final L2 error than Adam, SGD or quasi-Newton; Wang et al. [34] show that second-order methods implicitly align conflicting loss-term gradients and that the SOAP optimiser gives 2-10x accuracy gains across ten PDE benchmarks; Kiyani et al. [30] report orders-of-magnitude gains from self-scaled BFGS and Broyden variants without adaptive weights; Cheng and Na [57] pose training as trust-region SQP to counter ill-conditioning directly. For an 11 283-parameter network the cost objection largely vanishes, so this is the most transferable branch of the corpus, and the least exploited by this project, which has used only Adam and L-BFGS.

**Multi-stage networks are the only route with evidence to machine precision.** Wang and Lai [35] observe that single networks "struggle to reduce the prediction errors below O(1e-5)" and show that fitting the residual of one stage with a fresh network, stage after stage, brings both regression and PINN errors to nearly O(1e-16) in double precision; Ng, Wang and Lai [58] initialise each stage from the residual's spectrum; the same group's later work drives PDE residuals to round-off with gradient-normalised re-weighting [59] and full-matrix Gauss-Newton [60]. None of these evaluates an ODE system, but the method is problem-agnostic and this project's residual floor of 4.6e-4 in float32 is the regime [35] is designed to leave.

**Residual and error are linked by stability, so a small residual is necessary, not sufficient.** Mishra and Molinaro [61] bound the generalisation error by the training residual plus a collocation term through the PDE's stability; De Ryck and Mishra [62] give the same structure for Kolmogorov equations; Liu, Huang and Protopapas [63] give residual-based error bounds for most linear ODEs and some nonlinear ones without training assumptions. The practical content for this project is that the 30x error growth from t < 1 to t > 6 with a nearly flat residual profile is what a stability constant that grows with the window predicts, and it is why the error, not the loss, must be the reported metric.

**Adam's loss spikes are unexplained in the retrieved literature.** The Expert's Guide [25] prescribes Adam at 1e-3 with exponential decay of 0.9 every 2000 steps; Fazliani et al. [22] use the same and then L-BFGS until stall, finding 30k Adam plus 10k L-BFGS best of 40k; Anagnostopoulos et al. [64] describe first-order PINN training as drift, then diffusion, then a "diffusion equilibrium" in which sample-wise gradients align. No retrieved work characterises the periodic 10-100x loss spikes this project sees under Adam with linear decay. [unconfirmed: no work retrieved in this direction]

## 8. Critics and alternatives: when a PINN is the wrong tool

This branch bounds the recommendations. The critical literature is consistent that PINNs do not compete with classical solvers on forward problems, and the alternatives that do compete on ODEs replace gradient descent, not the physics loss.

Grossmann et al. [65] find the finite element method faster than PINNs in solve time by two to three orders on 1D/2D Poisson, five to six orders on 1D Allen-Cahn and two orders on 1D Schrödinger, with lower error, PINNs winning only on evaluation time; Chuang and Barba [66] report about 32 hours of PINN training to match a 16x16 finite-difference run of under 20 seconds and a failure to capture vortex shedding; McGreivy and Hakim [67] find that 79% (60 of 76) of papers claiming to beat a numerical method compared against a weak baseline; Hao et al. [68] find that vanilla PINNs solve only 10 of 22 PINNacle tasks at a 10% L2 threshold and that the long-time heat and Navier-Stokes tasks fail at roughly 100% error, with three repeats per configuration; Zhang et al. [69] summarise the root causes as poor multi-scale approximation, ill-conditioning from PDE losses and weak convergence theory. For a three-state ODE that DOP853 integrates to 1e-10 in milliseconds, these results mean the project's contribution cannot be "a better solver"; it can be a study of how a PINN behaves and of what makes it behave, which is what the failure-mode literature in Sections 4 and 5 is.

Among alternatives that keep the equation but drop the optimiser, Dwivedi and Srinivasan [41] solve the PINN least-squares problem in closed form with a random single hidden layer (PIELM); Dong and Li [70] extend this with domain decomposition and block time-marching, reporting errors and training time orders of magnitude below PINNs; Fabiani et al. [42] use random projection networks on stiff ODE initial-value problems and report beating ode15s, ode23t and ode23s in accuracy at comparable cost; Datar et al. [43] combine random features with causality in a gradient-free "Frozen-PINN" that beats state-of-the-art PINNs often by several orders on eight PDE benchmarks. Cardoso-Bihlo and Bihlo [71] instead keep the optimiser and project the output onto the system's invariant manifolds, reporting that exactly conservative PINNs "vastly outperform" non-conservative ones; this is directly relevant because Lorenz-1960 has two quadratic invariants and this project measures their drift at 6.7e-4 and 2.8e-4 against 3e-10 for the reference. Raissi et al. [44] discrete-time Runge-Kutta PINNs and Nagesh et al. [72] Taylor-model PINNs hybridise the network with a time-stepper; Kashyap et al. [45] report neural-ODE and universal-differential-equation fits to Lorenz-63 that break down near t = 15 after training on [0, 8]. No retrieved work compares PINN, PIELM and neural-ODE head to head on long-time ODE integration. [unconfirmed: no work retrieved in this direction]

On reproducibility, Krishnapriyan et al. [1] average over at least ten seeds and report that curriculum learning reduces error variance; PINNacle [68] reports mean and standard deviation over three repeats; Grossmann et al. [65] average timings over ten runs; hyperparameter studies exist [73][74][75] but none reports seed variance on ODE systems. This project's earlier [0, 1] sweep varied seeds for two architectures only, and every [0, 10] result above is one seed.

## 9. Synthesis discussion

Three cross-branch patterns emerge.

**The corpus has converged on a diagnosis and diverged on the remedy.** Sections 4 and 5 show four independent lines (fixed points [6][7], causality [8][9], propagation [16][38], cheating [37]) describing one failure, and Section 7 adds a fifth reading of the late-stage floor [4][5]. But the remedies split by what the authors treat as the binding constraint: the Perdikaris group treats optimisation order (causal weighting) and conditioning (modified MLP, second-order alignment) as central and validates on chaotic PDEs; the Karniadakis-adjacent work (Penwarden, Daw, Anagnostopoulos) treats collocation geometry and sweeping as central; the Graz group (Rohrhofer, Babic, Steger) treats the ODE's phase-space structure as central and validates on low-dimensional ODEs; the precision camp (Xu, Shao, Wang and Lai) treats numerics as central. The project's observations fit the Graz diagnosis best and the precision prescription best, which is a combination no single paper tests.

**Every headline gain is a package.** Causal weighting in [8] comes with Fourier features, a modified MLP, RWF and a learning-rate schedule [25]; SAFE-NET's [22] one hidden layer comes with Fourier features and an Adam-then-L-BFGS schedule; the multi-stage results [35] presuppose float64. This project's two single-knob ablations (normalisation alone, L-BFGS alone in float32) both failed to help, and the literature would have predicted both failures. The methodological consequence for the FYDP is that ablations should add a package and then remove pieces, not add pieces one at a time to a vanilla baseline.

**The evidence is PDE-heavy and the project is an ODE.** Of 63 works, the ones evaluating on a low-dimensional ODE are [6][7][8][23][37][42][45][46][47][48][63][71], and of those only [8], [23] and [48] touch a Lorenz system, all Lorenz-63. Nothing was retrieved on Lorenz-1960. That is not a weakness of the search but a finding: the project is in an empty cell, which raises its value and lowers the confidence with which any PDE result can be transferred to it.

## 10. Open problems and directions

1. **The [architecture x fixed-point] cell is empty.** All retrieved fixes for fixed-point optima act on the loss, the window or the sampling ([6][7][8][16]); no work asks whether an output parameterisation can exclude equilibria, for example by building the invariant manifolds of [71] into the trial function so that a flat curve at a fixed point is not representable. For Lorenz-1960, whose invariants are quadratic, this is concrete.
2. **The scaling of the hard-constraint factor with window length is unstudied.** Sections 6 and 9 show the requirement N(0) = T f(u0) under the normalised factor; Lagaris [52] uses the unnormalised form; nobody has compared g = t - t0, g = (t - t0)/T and g = 1 - exp(-(t - t0)/tau) on a long-window ODE. This is a one-line change with a measurable prediction (output range should stop growing with T).
3. **Precision-induced stall versus fixed-point optimum has not been tested on one system.** Xu et al. [5] and Rohrhofer et al. [6] make incompatible claims about what a plateau is; this project's data suggest they describe different stages (plateau vs floor). A float64 rerun of the same seed, checking whether the epoch-8000 escape moves, would decide it for one system.
4. **No head-to-head of PINN, PIELM/random-projection and neural ODE on long-time integration** was retrieved (Section 8), even though [42] and [70] claim orders-of-magnitude advantages for the least-squares methods and [45] shows neural ODEs breaking down at moderate horizons.
5. **Seed variance on ODE PINNs is unreported** outside benchmark side-protocols ([1][65][68]). Every [0, 10] number in this project is one seed; the [26] finding that some architectures diverge across seeds makes this the cheapest missing experiment.
6. **Adam loss spikes in PINN training have no published characterisation** (Section 7). Whether they are a symptom of ill-conditioning [4] or of the drift-diffusion transition [64] is untested.
7. **Lorenz-1960 itself is absent from the literature.** The nearest works are Lorenz-63 with causal training [8], shallow networks [23] and piecewise networks [48]. A study that places Lorenz-1960's periodic, two-invariant dynamics against Lorenz-63's chaotic dynamics under the same PINN recipe would be the first.

## 11. Conclusion

**RQ1.** The long-horizon failure observed here is the fixed-point local optimum that Rohrhofer et al. [6] describe, reached because the mean-over-time loss does not respect causality [8][9] and does not distinguish a flat equilibrium curve from the solution once the bend near t = 0 is a small fraction of the window. The remedies with the strongest evidence are causal weighting, validated on Lorenz-63 as part of a full training recipe, and time-window decomposition, validated across many time-dependent problems and, in this project, the only remedy that removed the plateau; each has a cost, respectively a need for the surrounding recipe and error accumulation across windows. The fixed-point regulariser of [7] is the most targeted remedy and the least validated for this system. Error growth with t is not a failure but the stability constant of the ODE acting on a flat residual, and it is why error against the reference, not loss, is the metric.

**RQ2.** For a three-state ODE the architecture evidence favours a shallow network with a Fourier feature map over the deep PDE recipes, and rejects input normalisation without a feature map, which this project confirmed. The accuracy floor of about 1e-3 on [0, 10] is imposed by Adam in float32: L-BFGS cannot take a step at that loss in float32 for reasons both the conditioning [4] and the precision [5] literature explain, and every method that reports going below it, float64 with a quasi-Newton or Newton finisher, natural gradient, or multi-stage networks, is affordable at eleven thousand parameters and untried here.

**RQ3.** The recipe the evidence supports, in the order the evidence is strongest: switch to float64 and finish with L-BFGS or NNCG (strong); keep raw t or, if normalising, add Fourier features (strong on mechanism); adopt Lagaris's unnormalised g = t - t0 in the hard constraint (mechanistic, this project only); use causal weighting with eps annealed from a normalised block loss, inside that recipe rather than on a vanilla network (medium); keep marching in reserve for windows where the single network still plateaus, with hard continuity at the joints (medium); report at least five seeds (the literature's own standard where it exists). The evidence runs out at exactly the points the project is positioned to supply: no published study covers Lorenz-1960, none scales the hard-constraint factor with the window, and none tests the precision and fixed-point explanations of a plateau on the same system.

The contribution of this survey is the mapping in Section 9: eight symptoms from one small PINN, each assigned to a mechanism with independent convergent evidence and to a remedy with a graded transfer claim, together with the observation that the field's headline gains are packages whose parts fail alone, which this project's two single-knob ablations illustrate.
